Interactive Anatomy

Anatomy of a Phishing Link

Scammers manipulate URL structures to trick the human eye. Study how malicious actors disguise domains vs how legitimate URLs are constructed.

Four Common URL Deception Techniques

Always read URLs from the first single forward slash (/) backwards to find the TRUE registered domain!

1. Subdomain Stuffing (Deceptive Brand Prefixes) High Risk
https://paypal.com.account-verify.security-login.attacker-portal.xyz/signin.php

How the trick works: The attacker places paypal.com in the subdomain. The browser does not connect to PayPal; it connects to the actual root domain attacker-portal.xyz!

2. Homograph & Typosquatting Attacks Critical Threat
https://pаypal.com (Punycode: xn--pypal-4ve.com)

How the trick works: The first 'а' in pаypal.com is a Cyrillic character (U+0430), not standard Latin 'a'. To human eyes they look identical, but they route to completely different servers.

3. HTTP Basic Authentication Credential Trick (@ Symbol) High Risk
https://google.com@malicious-phish.net/login

How the trick works: According to internet URL standards, everything before the @ symbol is treated as a username/password, and the browser navigates directly to malicious-phish.net!

4. Disposable & High-Abuse Top-Level Domains (TLDs) High Risk
http://usps-parcel-track28.top/clearance.php

How the trick works: Attackers buy dirt-cheap or free domains under TLDs such as .top, .xyz, .buzz, .tk, or .fit because they can be abandoned and replaced within minutes after being blacklisted.

Module 01

Introduction to Phishing

Demystifying one of the most common and damaging digital threats targeting students and organizations worldwide.

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a form of social engineering where cybercriminals masquerade as legitimate organizations (such as schools, banks, social networks, or tech companies) to manipulate victims into revealing confidential information.

Key Insight: Just like an angler dangles bait to catch a fish, an attacker dangles a fake lure—such as a fake urgent message—to hook unsuspecting users.

Why is It Dangerous?

A single careless click can have severe ripple effects:

  • Identity Theft: Attackers steal your SSN, student ID, and birth date to open fraudulent credit lines.
  • Financial Loss: Direct draining of bank accounts or credit card fraud.
  • Account Hijacking: Unauthorized takeover of email, gaming profiles, and social accounts.
  • Malware & Ransomware: Silent installation of keyloggers that record every keystroke.

Why Everyone Needs Awareness

Firewalls and antivirus scanners protect machines, but they cannot protect a human mind from psychological manipulation.

Students are prime targets: Scammers frequently target students with fake scholarship grants, urgent campus portal resets, fake tuition refunds, and student loan relief scams.
Module 02

How Phishing Works

Understanding the attack lifecycle and the underlying social engineering mechanics that trick human psychology.

🧠 The Psychology of Social Engineering

Social engineering is the art of manipulating people so they voluntarily give up confidential information or perform security-compromising actions. Instead of hacking complex computer software, cybercriminals hack the human mind by exploiting natural emotional triggers:

🚨 Fear & Urgency

"Your account will be deleted in 24 hours unless you verify immediately!" Creates panic so you don't stop to think.

🎁 Greed & Curiosity

"You won an unreleased iPhone!" or "Look who was caught on camera!" Baits users into impulsive clicks.

🏛️ Authority & Trust

Impersonating school principals, university IT administrators, police, or bank directors to command obedience.

🤝 Helpfulness & Sympathy

Posing as a stranded friend needing emergency funds or a student asking you to vote in a school contest.

The 5 Typical Stages of a Phishing Attack

1

Stage 1: Target Reconnaissance & Research

The attacker chooses their targets. For mass attacks, they scrape thousands of public student emails. For targeted attacks, they research social media profiles (LinkedIn, Instagram) to find your school, professors, and friends.

2

Stage 2: Weaponization & Setup

The attacker registers a deceptive lookalike domain (e.g., portal-univ-edu.xyz instead of univ.edu), builds an exact pixel-by-pixel clone of the real login page, and crafts convincing email templates with real logos.

3

Stage 3: Distribution & Baiting

The lure is cast! The attacker sends fake emails, SMS messages (Smishing), social media direct messages, or places automated phone calls (Vishing) embedded with urgency.

4

Stage 4: Deception & Exploitation

The unsuspecting victim clicks the link and arrives at the counterfeit web page. The victim types their username, password, or One-Time Password (OTP), believing they are logging in safely.

5

Stage 5: Exfiltration & Harm

The attacker captures the submitted credentials in real-time, takes over the account, accesses private emails/grades, drains funds, or leverages the compromised mailbox to phish other students.

Module 03

Common Types of Phishing

Attackers evolve their tactics across different communication mediums. Learn to recognize each form.

1. Email Phishing

The most common mass attack. Deceptive emails sent indiscriminately to thousands of recipients mimicking trusted brands (Netflix, Amazon, Google, Banks) asking you to "update billing" or "restore access".

2. Spear Phishing

A laser-focused, customized attack. The attacker targets a specific individual or team using personal details—such as mentioning your current course syllabus, your professor's actual name, or ongoing student club events.

3. Smishing (SMS Phishing)

Phishing conducted via text messages (SMS). Common lures include fake package delivery failure notices (USPS/FedEx), urgent bank security alerts, or fake toll road unpaid fines containing malicious shortlinks.

4. Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Attacks carried out via phone calls or VoIP. Scammers impersonate tax agencies, bank fraud investigators, or tech support agents demanding immediate payment via wire transfer or gift cards to resolve a fake crisis.

5. Clone Phishing

Attackers take a real, previously delivered legitimate email (like a university receipt or assignment handout), clone its layout and copy, but replace the attached file or hyperlinks with malware and credential stealers.

6. Social Media Phishing

Involves fake social profiles, compromised friend accounts, fake giveaways, or rogue quiz apps on Instagram, TikTok, Discord, and Snapchat designed to steal session tokens, passwords, or personal identity details.

Module 04

How to Identify Phishing: Common Warning Signs

Train your critical thinking. Look out for these 7 key forensic indicators whenever reviewing incoming messages.

⚠️

1. Urgent or Threatening Language

Phrases like "Account suspended in 24 hours!", "Legal action pending", or "Act now or lose access" are designed to short-circuit calm rational judgment.

📧

2. Suspicious Sender Addresses

Inspect the actual email domain after the '@' symbol. Look for subtle misspellings (typosquatting) like @micros0ft.com, @support-google.net, or unrelated generic domains.

📎

3. Unexpected Attachments

Unsolicited files ending in .exe, .scr, .vbs, .zip, or macro-enabled documents (.docm, .xlsm) frequently carry malware payloads.

🔗

4. Strange or Shortened Links

Hover over hyperlinks before clicking! Displayed text may say paypal.com while the actual hyperlink destination directs to an untrusted domain or generic URL shortener.

🔑

5. Requests for Passwords, OTPs, or Banking Info

Legitimate organizations, schools, and banks will never ask you to verify passwords, PINs, or One-Time Passwords (OTP) via email, text, or phone.

✍️

6. Spelling & Grammatical Mistakes

Awkward phrasing, inconsistent fonts, broken punctuation, or generic greetings like "Dear Valued Customer" instead of your actual student name.

🎁

7. Offers Too Good To Be True

Unsolicited notices claiming you have won a $1,000 lottery, a random student grant, or an exclusive high-paying work-from-home job with no interview required.

Scenario 1: Campus IT Emergency Account Suspension

A student receives this alarming email in their college inbox during final exam week.

From: Campus IT Support <it-support@univ-portal-security-alert.org🚩 Red Flag 1>
To: alex.student@state-university.edu
Subject: URGENT: Your Campus Account Will Be Terminated in 24 Hours!🚩 Red Flag 2

Dear Valued University User,🚩 Red Flag 3


Our server maintenance detected suspicious login attempts from an unauthorized IP address. To prevent permanent deactivation of your student email, course access, and grade history, you are required to verify your student credentials immediately.

Failure to confirm within 24 hours will result in permanent deletion of your university records.

Click Here to Validate Student ID & Password 🚩 Red Flag 4

Destination preview: http://portal-verify-login.xyz/auth?student=alex🚩 Red Flag 5

Please find attached Security_Update_Patch.exe for your laptop.🚩 Red Flag 6


Warm regards,
Campus IT Service Desk
Official Automated Dispatcher

1
Suspicious Spoofed Domain

The sender address is @univ-portal-security-alert.org instead of the university's authentic domain (@state-university.edu). Anyone can register a fake lookalike domain.

2
Manufactured Artificial Panic & Urgency

Threatening account deletion in 24 hours panics students during exam periods, pushing them to act before verifying with their school IT office.

3
Impersonal / Generic Salutation

Universities know your student name and address you directly (e.g., "Dear Alex,"). Phishing emails use mass generic greetings like "Dear Valued University User".

4 & 5
Deceptive Destination Link

The link leads to an unverified top-level domain (.xyz) over insecure HTTP instead of the university's official single-sign-on portal.

6
Dangerous Executable Attachment (.exe)

Official IT departments never distribute software updates as raw .exe files in emails. This is a trojan or infostealer.

Scenario 2: Package Delivery Smishing

A student receives an unexpected text message on their smartphone regarding an urgent parcel hold.

9:41 AM +1 (833) 492-9102🚩 Red Flag 1 📶 100%
USPS Notification: Your parcel #US-83921 cannot be dispatched due to an incomplete street address.

Action required within 12 hours🚩 Red Flag 2 to avoid return to sender.

Please confirm your delivery address and pay $1.95 redelivery clearance fee🚩 Red Flag 3 at:
https://usps-parcel-dispatch9.top/track🚩 Red Flag 4
1
Random Unregistered Number

USPS uses official 5-digit SMS short codes (like 28777), never arbitrary 10-digit toll-free or international numbers.

2
False Urgency

Creating an artificial 12-hour deadline to prevent victims from calmly checking whether they actually ordered a package.

3
Micro-Fee Credit Card Bait

Asking for a tiny fee ($1.95) makes victims think "it's only $2, no big deal!" But the fake payment portal captures your complete card number, expiration, and CVV code.

4
Lookalike Fraudulent Domain

The genuine postal service website is usps.com. The domain here is usps-parcel-dispatch9.top, an inexpensive fraudulent domain registered overseas.

Scenario 3: Lookalike Google Workspace Sign-In Page

A student clicks a link to view a "Shared Class Project" and lands on this spoofed login screen.

🔒 http://accounts.google.com.user-verify-session9.biz/auth/login🚩 Red Flag 1
Google

Sign in to view shared document

Continue to Google Docs Cloud

⚠️ Also enter your 6-digit Authenticator OTP code to proceed: 🚩 Red Flag 2
1
Subdomain Deception & Insecure Protocol

Look carefully at the domain structure! Attackers place accounts.google.com at the front as a subdomain, but the real registered domain before the slash is user-verify-session9.biz. Google never hosts sign-ins on .biz domains!

2
Real-Time 2FA / OTP Interception (Reverse Proxy Phishing)

Modern phishing toolkits (like Evilginx) prompt for your OTP simultaneously. As soon as you type your code, the hacker forwards it to Google to capture your authenticated session cookie!

Scenario 4: Compromised Friend Social Media DM

A direct message arrives at late night from a mutual classmate you follow.

SJ

Sarah Jenkins (@sarah_j22)

Hey Alex!! 😱 OMG is this really you in this video?!🚩 Red Flag 1 Why did someone upload this about you?? LOL you look crazy in it 😂
Look before they take it down:
https://bit.ly/video-viral-leak-92🚩 Red Flag 2

1
High-Emotion Social Bait

Playing on embarrassment and curiosity ("is this you in this video?"). This is a classic automated bot script used when an account has been hijacked.

2
Obfuscated Shortlink (bit.ly)

Shortened links hide the actual malicious URL destination. Clicking this takes you to a fake Instagram login page that steals your password.

3
Social Pressure ("Everyone at school is talking about it")

Creates FOMO (fear of missing out) and social embarrassment to prevent you from contacting your friend via another app or in person to verify.

Module 06

Phishing vs. Legitimate Messages

A quick forensic reference guide comparing genuine communications against deceptive phishing attempts.

Evaluation Feature ✓ Legitimate Message ✗ Phishing Attempt
Sender Address Matches the authentic registered domain exactly (e.g., @apple.com or @university.edu). Verified sender badges where supported. Misspelled domains, hyphens, or random domains (e.g., @apple-support-verify.com, @gmail.com).
Salutation / Greeting Personalized with your verified first and last name or account number (e.g., "Hello Alex Johnson"). Generic, impersonal greetings like "Dear Customer", "Dear Student", or just your email prefix.
Sense of Urgency Calm, informative tone. Offers reasonable timelines and clear contact options without aggressive deadlines. Extreme artificial urgency: "Account suspended within 24 hours!", "Arrest warrant issued", panic-inducing threats.
Destination Links Clean, transparent URLs that match official domains. Hyperlink text and destination match identically. Mismatched URLs, lookalike subdomains (accounts.google.com.evil-site.org), or masked shortlinks (bit.ly).
Request for Sensitive Data Never requests passwords, PINs, or One-Time Passwords (OTPs) via email, chat, or phone. Explicitly requests passwords, OTPs, Social Security Numbers, or credit card CVV details to "verify" identity.
Attachments Standard PDF or document formats clearly anticipated by the recipient with clear digital signatures. Unexpected executable files (.exe, .scr, .bat, .vbs) or macro-enabled documents (.docm).
Grammar & Branding Polished copy reviewed by professional communications teams with high-resolution, sharp corporate logos. Frequent spelling typos, awkward syntax, broken punctuation, or blurry low-resolution stolen graphics.
Verification Path Provides independent customer support numbers and directs users to log in directly via the official app. Insists that you only use the provided phone number or embedded button and warns against leaving the page.
